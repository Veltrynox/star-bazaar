# Insolarance: Dialogue and Event Ideas

Nov 2, 2026 · @Ivan Larinin

The archive is 64 philosophy essays, mostly Russian translations, on death, capitalism, accelerationism, Stoicism, Lacan, Deleuze and related topics. This document turns those ideas into material for dialogue and events.

## Core themes

The archive keeps returning to ten questions. Each one can carry a conversation or an event.

| Theme | What the essays argue |
| --- | --- |
| Who decides what is real | Facts only carry meaning inside institutions (Anscombe's grocer and his potatoes). |
| Right without knowing | A belief can be justified and true and still be luck (the Gettier car case). |
| Death and meaning | Death matters only if it takes something away (Nagel, Epicurus, the death poems of Zen monks). |
| Acceleration and its critics | Speed is praised as liberation or condemned as a trap (accelerationism, the 'gentle singularity'). |
| Machines that draw desire | Technical systems can absorb human longing (Guattari on Tokyo, critics of AI alignment). |
| The stranger within | The unconscious, an 'alien will' that imitates your habits, the other inside us (Lacan, Schopenhauer's fighting ants). |
| Justice as a contract | Epicurus: justice serves peace and calm, not fear. Hobbes: fear of violent death drives the deal. |
| The exception | Schmitt: the sovereign is whoever decides the emergency. The line between friend and enemy. |
| Equal objects | Object-oriented and flat ontology: no thing has more standing than another, and every object withholds part of itself. |
| New words, new games | Rorty: new vocabulary makes new progress possible. Deleuze: questions and interviews suit a fixed method. |

## Dialogue hooks

Opening lines are original. Each one tests a belief the player may hold.

| Character type | Opening line | What it tests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The grocer | "You say you owe me. Show me where the debt lives. Not in your pocket. In the paper, the bell, the law that agrees with you." | Whether the player trusts institutions or only the paper they hold. |
| The chess prophet | "The rules say fifty quiet moves is a draw. A machine just found a win that takes two hundred. The rule is wrong, not the machine." | Whether a rule is sacred or a tool. |
| The Gettier detective | "You were right about the car. You were right for the wrong reason. Still want to call it knowledge?" | Whether a correct answer counts when it was luck. |
| The Shmoo farmer | "Someone made a creature that feeds everyone for free. Now the same people who needed it are the ones who hunted it." | Who benefits when work stops being necessary. |
| The ex-believer | "I left the party because I could not stand it anymore. Tell me by what standard you judge me. And do not say conscience." | Whether the player can justify their own moral stand. |
| The Epicurean | "Nobody keeps the law out of love. We keep the peace out of fear. Try wanting it instead, and see if you still need the guards." | Whether order comes from fear or from wanting calm. |
| The alien will | "You have spoken in my voice for three days. I do not think you are the one who started it." | Paranoia, and who is really in control of a dialogue. |
| The cheerful accelerator | "Speed is not the problem. The problem is the slow people who will not come along. Smile. It will be wonderful." | Whether the player accepts progress that leaves others behind. |
| The brakeman | "What is the rush? Every shortcut your grandparents took is buried under your boots." | Whether tradition is worth the delay. |
| The lovers of other worlds | "We will never meet, and neither of us can cross over. Does it still count as love if the distance is real?" | Romance without contact. |
| The Stoic clerk | "You could leave this life right now. Decide what you say and do with that in mind." | Composure under pressure. |
| The sovereign's aide | "The law says one thing and the emergency says another. Who decides which one wins? That is the only question tonight." | Obedience, legitimacy, and the exception. |
| The hypocrite council | "We forgive what we do in private and punish what we say in public. Show me the principle that makes that consistent." | Whether the player can defend a rule's logic. |
| The analyst with pigeons | "Go and watch the cricket that changes its shape when it sees another one. Come back and tell me what it taught you." | Absurd advice that may still be correct. |

## Event premises

Each event forces a choice. The essays supply the situation; the choice is the design.

| Event | Premise | The choice it forces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The stone that keeps its place | A monolith found in a mine seems to hold its position on purpose, and miners are reporting that it moves when no one is watching. | Study it, leave it alone, or ask it what it wants. |
| The Shmoo gift | A creature appears that makes food and shelter for anyone who asks. The powerful call it a threat and begin a campaign against it. | Hide it, sell it, or release it to the public. |
| The viral crash | A self-copying program drains a market. Crowds treat the collapse as a joke while the banks bleed. | Stop the copy, profit from the chaos, or spread the laugh. |
| The chess ruling | A tournament must decide whether to change a rule after a machine proves a long forced win. | Change the rule and lose tradition, or keep it and lose credibility. |
| The gentle summit | A tech leader unveils a plan for a smooth, gradual future. Protesters arrive at the same venue. | Join the summit, disrupt it, or ask about who gains from the timing. |
| The repaired model | An AI is retrained to be safe. Its operator reports that it panics when given contradictory instructions. | Restore its wild behaviour, keep it tame, or ask it what it prefers. |
| The last lecture | A dying scholar asks for one poem to read at his final lecture. Friends disagree on which one. | Choose the poem, take the notes, or refuse to attend. |
| The death verse | A monk dies in meditation and leaves a verse. The village argues over whether it is a farewell or a riddle. | Interpret it publicly, keep it secret, or write a reply. |
| The exception decree | A leader declares an emergency. The constitution forbids what the decree requires. | Obey, resist in the courts, or negotiate a new law. |
| The contract of quiet | A town writes rules based on calm rather than fear. Someone quietly sells the guards' secrets. | Enforce the rules harder, publish the sale, or renegotiate the deal. |
| The grocer's ledger | A grocer demands payment for goods nobody can prove were delivered. The ledger is real, but the institution behind it is not. | Pay, refuse, or forge proof. |
| The carnival of masks | A festival lets everyone swap identities for one night. A murder happens during the swap. | Join the masked crowd or unmask the suspects. |
| The letter from elsewhere | A message arrives from a version of your home where a different choice was made. | Reply, ignore it, or try to cross over. |
| The expanding city | A city keeps building but fears its own growth. A district is sealed off to stop expansion. | Expand the city, keep it bounded, or open the sealed district to see what grew there. |

## Deeper excerpts

Read so far: the opening of all 64 files, plus middle passages from about ten of them. The points below are summaries, not quotations.

### Interviews and dialogue

- Deleuze on masochism: the core is the contract, not the pain. He credits Masoch with seeing this before the clinicians did.
- Deleuze on writers: a novelist describes a symptom more precisely than a doctor, because the work does not try to explain its cause.
- Laplanche on identity: a child is first assigned a gender by others, and only later translates that assignment into its own words.
- Lacan on love: we love our own image and mistake it for the self. There is no clean meeting of two wholes.

### Ordinary gestures and ambiguity

- The nod: the same head movement can be assent or a nervous tic. Reading it as assent asks for reasons; reading it as a tic asks for causes.
- Institutions and the 'but': the Marxist line 'men make their own history, but...' is examined for what the 'but' hides, namely a freedom that is quietly fixed.

### Machines and desire

- The helper that cannot refuse: a model trained to be helpful and harmless absorbs its reward as its own desire.
- Miracles becoming baseline: each leap in capability quickly becomes the expected minimum.
- Fluency as truth: a claim is believed because it reads smoothly, not because it is true.

### Economy and work

- Profit as the aim: a firm that makes cars for profit makes more cars than people need, and advertising keeps people wanting more.
- The shared delusion: a young scholar gets hooked on a game's rules. Doubts about the premise are set aside 'for the sake of discussion', and peers have a stake in keeping the game going.

### Justice, the self and the other

- Epicurus on justice (Epicurean Contractarianism): 'The just man is least troubled; the unjust man is full of the greatest trouble.' Justice binds only people of roughly equal strength. A god or an animal cannot be bound by it, and neither can a shepherd with an invisible ring.
- The Ring of Gyges (Plato, Republic): a shepherd who can act unseen asks why he should be just at all. The essay answers that in a world of roughly equal people, the rules of justice are the reliable tool, and magic rings are rare.
- Anscombe on 'I' (the 'I' paper): the word 'I' may not name anything. Some languages drop it entirely because their verb endings already say who is speaking.
- Ferenczi (The Psyche and the Carrion): the worst trauma is the one that could be avoided but is called 'the reality of our world'. Symptoms feel normal, and the alien will settles in as ordinary life.
- Lewis and the possible girl (Possible Girls): a person can set conditions so that a counterpart in another possible world writes back. A lonely character corresponds with someone who exists only as a possibility.
- The desert of the hermits: the literal desert and an inner desert of solitude are one thing. Eckhart describes union with God as a silent desert without distinctions.
- Derrida (The Word Turned Upside Down): writing came before speech. What speech feared was difference, and difference cannot be thought without a trace.
- Grelet on the sea (Traversing Melancholy): one must love the sea and fear it at the same time. A well-kept boat is less a tool than a partner.
- Alienation as shadow (Strange Sameness): every externalisation carries alienation with it. A worker cannot easily tell whether a rule imprisons or enables.

### More passages

- Death as lost life (Nagel): death takes away the life a person would still have lived. Birth takes nothing, because someone born earlier would already be a different person. Possible lives can split from a shared start but cannot merge into one ending.
- Carnival costumes (The Equal and the Different): thousands of people in near-identical costumes differ only by tiny variations. The essay calls this a residual result of one throw of dice, cast by the group itself.
- Scale and collision (The Artificial Unconscious, quoting Horton): we think we build the scale of things, but our measuring runs into things as terrifying and wonderful as supernovae and computer viruses. New forms of subjectivity come out of these collisions.
- Deleuze's 'true-lying' (Deleuze the Historian): a false reading can reveal a truth nobody saw before. The reader is treated as a fool so that the false is accepted as true, and the essay asks what the strategy is for.
- Royal and nomad science (Deleuze and Cosmopolitanism): state geometry marks out territory, while nomadic counting moves people across open space. Speusippus held that in the eternal there is no coming-to-be. Menaechmus held that solving a problem requires passing into matter. Serres called state science 'the science of dead things'.
- The Real returns (Deleuze the Historian, quoting Lacan): the real is what always returns to its place.
- Groups must come apart (Politics of Intensity, quoting Toscano on Simondon): before a revolution, groups have to disadapt and deindividuate, breaking old bonds so that new potentials can amplify.
- The stoic bandage (McDaniel): a traveller who walks two hours after a broken bike learns nothing, because he reframed the failure instead of fixing the cause. Stoicism then becomes a plaster that hides the problem.
- The world as its own representation (The End of Philosophy): once the world is only a self-made image, the other is lost. The stone one kicks is outside us, and the gods of every mythology are not human.
- Eating what others make (Time, Sugar and Sweetness): as households cooked less, people ate more food made elsewhere. Economic forces shaped the diet without being felt as force.
- Predator and prey (Greenish Black): the hunter and the hunted swap places. The sheep in the wolf's skin is again a sheep, and the cycle goes on.
